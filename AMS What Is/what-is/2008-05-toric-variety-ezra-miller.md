## Small mathematical units worth preserving

### 1. Polyhedron → toric variety

Miller starts with a convex polyhedron `P` and describes the corresponding toric variety `X_P`. The point is not merely that a polyhedron is a convenient picture: several otherwise different definitions of toric varieties meet in this construction.

### 2. Tori over the faces

Set-theoretically, the space can be viewed as pieces of the form

`F° × T^dim(F)`

for the relative interiors of the faces `F` of the polyhedron. As a point approaches the boundary of a face, part of the torus fiber collapses.

This is probably the cleanest animation seed in the article.

### 3. The interval becomes a sphere

For an interval `P`, the corresponding toric variety is a sphere. Over points in the interior sit circles; toward the two endpoints those circles shrink and collapse to the poles.

That is a complete visual explanation in itself and should be tested before attempting anything more elaborate.

### 4. Algebraic-torus orbit closure

For an `n`-dimensional complex toric variety, the algebraic torus `(C×)^n` acts on the variety and occurs as a dense open subset. Miller gives the compact formulation: a toric variety is an algebraic torus orbit closure.

### 5. Moment map

The quotient by the compact torus action recovers the polyhedron via the moment map. This gives a direct bridge between the algebraic variety and the polyhedral picture.

## Possible visual studies — not scripts

- Draw an interval; attach a circle over each interior point; shrink the circles continuously to points at the ends; reveal the sphere formed by the total family.
- Repeat in one dimension higher with a polygon and indicate how the torus dimension changes over faces, edges, and vertices.
- Put the polyhedron and toric variety side by side and animate the moment-map projection.
- Show a few monomial exponent vectors as lattice points, then their convex hull, to hint at the sparse-polynomial side of toric geometry.
