## What this file is

This book should not be treated as fifteen possible videos. It is hundreds of possible little geometric stories. The coordinates below are places to open the book and look for one detachable picture, question, example, construction, comparison, or theorem.

Status convention:

- `TOC-verified`: Berger's detailed table of contents gives this exact section title and starting page.
- `chapter-text`: a publisher-indexed chapter abstract or searchable text was also checked.
- Nothing here should be treated as a finished Short until the actual pages/figures are inspected.

## First pass: unusually visual fragments in the first 140 pages

These are probably the best place to begin because Berger deliberately starts from concrete geometry before moving into the abstract machinery.

### p. 4 — First variation and billiards

Section 1.2.3, **The First Variation Formula and Application to Billiards**. `TOC-verified`.

Possible picture: fix the incoming and outgoing endpoints, slide the bounce point along a wall, and display total path length. At the minimizing/stationary point the reflected path has equal incidence/reflection angles. The animation can make the variational statement visible before any formula is introduced.

### p. 12 — Curvature of a plane curve

Section 1.3.2, **Curvature**. `TOC-verified`.

Possible fragment: move along a drawn curve and attach the tangent plus an osculating circle/curvature indicator. This is small enough to become an explanation of what curvature is measuring rather than a survey of curve theory.

### p. 20 — Four Vertex Theorem

Section 1.4.2. `TOC-verified`.

Possible fragment: start from a smooth convex oval, plot curvature around the boundary, and mark maxima/minima. The visual question comes first: how few curvature extrema can a closed convex curve have?

### p. 24 — Heat shrinking of plane curves

Section 1.4.5, **Heat Shrinking of Plane Curves**. `TOC-verified`.

Possible fragment: evolve several irregular embedded loops under curve shortening and compare how corners/high-curvature regions move faster. This is particularly suited to an animation rather than prose.

### p. 30 — First attempt at inner geometry

Section 1.6.1, **Inner Geometry: a First Attempt**. `TOC-verified`.

Possible fragment: draw distances/geodesics on a sheet and then bend the sheet without stretching it. Ask which measurements belong to the surface itself and which depend on how it sits in space.

### p. 33 — Looking for shortest curves: geodesics

Section 1.6.2. `TOC-verified`.

Possible fragment: two points on plane/sphere/cylinder, several competing paths, then the shortest path selected. Avoid defining geodesics by formula first.

### p. 45 — Second fundamental form and principal curvatures

Section 1.6.3. `TOC-verified`.

Possible fragment: at one point of a saddle or ellipsoid, rotate a plane containing the normal and show the normal curvature changing with direction; then mark the extremal directions.

### p. 52 — Meaning of the sign of K

Section 1.6.4. `TOC-verified`.

Possible fragment: the same local construction on a sphere, plane/cylinder, and saddle. Show what positive/zero/negative Gaussian curvature looks like before giving a symbolic definition.

### p. 58 — Minimal surfaces

Section 1.6.6. `TOC-verified`.

Possible fragment: a boundary wire deforms while the spanning surface relaxes; distinguish "minimal" from "smallest imaginable surface" and isolate one local visual fact.

### p. 63 — Isoperimetric inequality in E³ à la Gromov

Section 1.6.8. `TOC-verified`.

This is also a direct Berger → Gromov bridge for the channel. Audit the pages carefully and use Berger's formulation/diagram rather than supplying a generic isoperimetric animation.

### pp. 70–98 — heat, waves, drums, spheres, billiards

The TOC contains **Shape of a Drum** (p. 78), the Faber–Krahn inequality (p. 81), inverse problems (p. 83), heat (p. 87), eigenfunctions (p. 90), spheres (p. 95), billiards in higher dimensions (p. 97), and wave versus heat equation (p. 98).

This is a whole cluster of separate visual questions. In particular: same-looking spectra for differently shaped domains; nodal patterns; heat diffusion versus oscillation; and billiard rays versus spectral data.

High-priority pictures from this cluster:

1. **Parallel transport**: carry one arrow around a loop and show the angular defect on sphere versus plane.
2. **Cut locus**: grow shortest geodesics from one point until uniqueness fails; sphere and ellipsoid are natural comparison cases.
3. **Bending without stretching**: preserve an intrinsic grid while extrinsic shape changes.
4. **Gauss map**: normals from a curved patch sweep area on the unit sphere.
5. **Geodesy angle correction**: a large terrestrial triangle visibly stops behaving like a planar triangle.

## Metric geometry and curvature — pp. 221–297

The chapter text begins with three concrete tasks: define distance as the infimum of lengths of connecting curves, ask whether the infimum is realized, and study the realizing curves. That makes a much better starting point than presenting the Riemannian metric tensor as unexplained notation.

Source for chapter abstract: https://ideas.repec.org/h/spr/sprchp/978-3-642-18245-7_6.html

Useful locators:

- p. 229 — convexity and small balls
- p. 233 — center of mass
- p. 235 — examples of geodesics
- p. 248 — exponential map
- p. 254 — nonpositive curvature
- p. 257 — triangle comparison theorems
- p. 267 — philosophy behind curvature bounds
- pp. 268–285 — injectivity radius, convexity radius, cut locus, Blaschke manifolds

Visual priority: **triangle comparison**. Put the same side-length/geodesic construction in positive, zero, and negative curvature and let the picture show what the curvature bound controls.

## What is the best metric? — pp. 499–541

Berger says the question "What is the best Riemannian structure on a given compact manifold?" was asked of him by René Thom around 1960; he later found Hopf had posed it earlier. That historical question is itself an excellent opening.
