## January — Jost Bürgi: finite differences in the complex plane

**Function/picture.** The calendar studies a rational function attached to a finite-difference stencil. Finite-difference formulas replace a derivative by a weighted combination of nearby function values; the complex phase portrait lets us inspect the poles, zeros, and geometry of the resulting characteristic rational function rather than treating the stencil as a row of coefficients.

**Story.** Jost/Jobst Bürgi was a clockmaker and instrument maker as well as a mathematical innovator. He developed logarithmic ideas independently of Napier, worked with Kepler, and is an early figure in finite-difference approximation. That is a useful story because numerical analysis did not begin with electronic computers: it grew out of the practical need to calculate tables, astronomy, and instruments.

**Video seeds.** Start with three or five sample points on a line and ask: “How can five function values pretend to be a derivative?” Then replace the weights by the associated complex rational function and reveal the phase portrait. A second video can move the stencil points and watch poles/zeros move.

**Reading.** [Jost Bürgi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jost_B%C3%BCrgi); [finite difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_difference); Bengt Fornberg, “Generation of Finite Difference Formulas on Arbitrarily Spaced Grids,” *Mathematics of Computation* 51 (1988), 699–706.

## February — Elwin Bruno Christoffel: Gauss-Christoffel quadrature

**Function/picture.** The month connects Gaussian quadrature with polynomial interpolation. Its featured phase portrait is an interpolation error `f(z)-p(z)` for `f(z)=exp(-z^3)`, using six quadrature/interpolation nodes for the weight `1/sqrt(1-x^2)`. The Chebyshev connection makes the nodes visible as part of a complex analytic object rather than merely six numbers on the real line.

**Story.** Christoffel worked across analysis, geometry, and numerical mathematics. His name now appears in Christoffel symbols, the Christoffel-Darboux formula, and Gauss-Christoffel quadrature. The lesson should focus on the recurring idea: choose special polynomial zeros so an approximation is much better than naïve equally spaced sampling.

**Video seeds.** “Why are these six points better than six evenly spaced points?” Show the real nodes first, then the complex error field. Mark the places where interpolation is exact. A follow-up can compare node choices by animating the error portrait.

**Reading.** [Elwin Bruno Christoffel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elwin_Bruno_Christoffel); [Gaussian quadrature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_quadrature); [Chebyshev polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebyshev_polynomials).

## March — Édouard Goursat: eigenvalues from contour integrals

**Function/picture.** The entry uses contour integration to build a rational filter for a nonlinear eigenvalue problem. A function of the form `b(z)=sum u_j/(t_j-z)` approximates the indicator of the region enclosed by a contour; the calendar uses a unit-square contour. The phase portrait turns the rational filter into a visible inside/outside separator.

**Story.** Goursat is strongly associated with the Cauchy-Goursat theorem. The modern numerical story is more interesting than a theorem-name biography: contour integration, usually taught as exact pure mathematics, can be used as an algorithm to isolate eigenvalues inside a chosen region.

**Video seeds.** Draw a square around several candidate eigenvalues and ask: “Can a contour behave like a search filter?” Then show the rational approximation lighting up the inside. Another video can deform the contour and show which eigenvalues remain selected.

**Reading.** [Édouard Goursat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Goursat); [Cauchy's integral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy%27s_integral_theorem); Wolf-Jürgen Beyn, “An integral method for solving nonlinear eigenvalue problems,” *Linear Algebra and its Applications* 436 (2012), 3839–3863.

## April — Trevor Pearcey: caustics, interference, and the Pearcey integral

**Function/picture.** The featured special function is the Pearcey integral,

`P(x,y) = integral exp(i(t^4 + y t^2 + x t)) dt`,

viewed with one variable complexified. It models the diffraction pattern near a cusp caustic. The phase portrait gives a direct route from an oscillatory integral to the complicated interference geometry it generates.

**Story.** Trevor Pearcey was both a mathematical physicist and a computing pioneer in Australia, closely associated with CSIRAC. The function bearing his name came from wave optics, not from an attempt to invent a pretty special function. That makes a good calendar-to-video transition: the visual complexity has a physical origin.

**Video seeds.** Start with light focused through a curved surface and a bright cusp. Then ask: “What function draws the universal pattern near this cusp?” Morph from a real interference plot to the complex phase portrait. A second Short can vary `y` and show the cusp catastrophe reorganize.

**Reading.** [Trevor Pearcey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trevor_Pearcey); [Pearcey integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearcey_integral); T. Pearcey, “The structure of an electromagnetic field in the neighbourhood of a cusp of a caustic,” *Philosophical Magazine* 37 (1946), 311–317.

## May — Constantin Carathéodory: prime ends and conformal boundaries

**Function/picture.** The calendar uses a conformal map from a slit/rayed domain to the disk. The subtlety is that ordinary boundary points do not carry enough information: approaching the same geometric point from two sides of a slit can mean two different boundary approaches. Carathéodory's prime ends repair this. The month picture is the phase portrait of the derivative of the conformal map.

**Story.** Carathéodory first trained and worked as a civil engineer, including work connected with the Suez Canal and Nile regulation, before switching to mathematics. His later work ranged across complex analysis, measure theory, variational calculus, thermodynamics, and more. For this lesson, the important story is that a seemingly technical definition—prime ends—solves a concrete ambiguity in what “the boundary” means to a conformal map.

**Video seeds.** Draw a slit disk and move two dots toward the same endpoint from opposite sides. Ask: “Are these really the same boundary point?” Then show their different images on the unit circle. Follow with the derivative phase portrait to reveal where the map stretches and turns.

**Reading.** [Constantin Carathéodory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantin_Carath%C3%A9odory); [prime end](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_end); [Riemann mapping theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_mapping_theorem).

## June — Maryam Mirzakhani: volumes of moduli spaces

**Function/picture.** Mirzakhani's recursion for Weil-Petersson volumes leads to explicit polynomials in boundary lengths. The calendar isolates a family of functions `F_(2k+1)(t)` whose coefficients involve even values of the Riemann zeta function and plots the case `k=3`. This is a useful bridge from a concrete complex function to the geometry of spaces whose points represent whole Riemann surfaces.

**Story.** Mirzakhani connected hyperbolic geometry, complex analysis, dynamics, and topology. Her 2007 *Inventiones* paper gave recursive formulas for volumes of moduli spaces of bordered hyperbolic surfaces. In 2014 she became the first woman awarded the Fields Medal. The video should not stop at that biography fact: the mathematical punch line is that the “space of all shapes of a surface” itself has a measurable volume, and a recursion computes it.

**Video seeds.** Begin with a torus and ask: “How big is the space of all possible hyperbolic tori?” Then replace individual surfaces by points in moduli space and end on the calendar's zeta-coefficient function. Another video can animate boundary lengths and the corresponding volume polynomial.

**Reading.** [Maryam Mirzakhani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryam_Mirzakhani); [moduli space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_space); Maryam Mirzakhani, “Simple geodesics and Weil-Petersson volumes of moduli spaces of bordered Riemann surfaces,” *Inventiones Mathematicae* 167 (2007), 179–222.

## July — Eduard Stiefel: optimal multiband filters

**Function/picture.** The problem is to construct a rational transfer function that stays near one target value on several passbands and another on several stopbands. Zolotarev solved the one-passband/one-stopband ancestor with elliptic functions; Stiefel and later work pushed the idea into more bands and higher-genus algebraic curves. The month shows a five-passband filter through the phase portrait of `1/2-G(z)`.

**Story.** Stiefel moved from algebraic topology into numerical and applied mathematics, founded ETH Zürich's Institute for Applied Mathematics, obtained Zuse's Z4, and helped build ERMETH, an early Swiss computer. His career makes the filter problem feel less like abstract approximation theory and more like a junction between topology, computation, and electrical engineering.

**Video seeds.** “Can one rational function pass five frequency bands and kill the rest?” Mark alternating real intervals, then reveal the phase portrait. A useful animation can increase the number of bands and show why genus enters the problem.

**Reading.** [Eduard Stiefel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduard_Stiefel); [elliptic filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_filter); E. Stiefel, “Le problème d’approximation dans la théorie des filtres électriques” (1961); A. B. Bogatyrev, “Chebyshev representation of rational functions,” *Sbornik: Mathematics* 201 (2010), 1579–1598.

## August — Rolf Nevanlinna: functions that keep the upper half-plane upstairs

**Function/picture.** A Nevanlinna/Herglotz function is analytic on the upper half-plane and has nonnegative imaginary part there. Such functions have a rigid integral representation. The calendar gives elementary examples and then plots a nested/composed example, making the upper-half-plane-preserving property visible directly in the coloring.

**Story.** Nevanlinna transformed the study of how meromorphic functions distribute their values. Here the narrower lesson is especially visual: an inequality on the imaginary part becomes a global color restriction on half the plane. The integral representation says that this visual constraint is not accidental; the whole class has a strong structural description.

**Video seeds.** Color the complex plane by argument and draw the real axis. Ask: “Can an analytic function send every point above this line to another point above the line?” Show `log z`, `tan z`, and a composition. The viewer can verify the condition by color before seeing the theorem.

**Reading.** [Rolf Nevanlinna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolf_Nevanlinna); [Nevanlinna function / Herglotz representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herglotz_representation_theorem); Lars Ahlfors, “Rolf Nevanlinna in memoriam,” *Acta Mathematica* 149 (1982).

## September — Walther Ritz: Ritz values and compressed eigenvalue problems

**Function/picture.** For a huge matrix, the Rayleigh-Ritz idea projects the eigenvalue problem onto a much smaller subspace. The eigenvalues of that compressed matrix are Ritz values. The calendar plots the characteristic polynomial of a 50-dimensional compression of the 500-by-500 Grcar matrix; its fifty zeros are the Ritz values.

**Story.** Walther Ritz died at only 31, but his variational ideas became basic tools in mathematical physics and numerical analysis. One surprising biographical connection: his only coauthor was Albert Einstein, in a short paper laying out opposing positions on electrodynamics. The lesson should center on compression: a giant eigenproblem can be interrogated through a much smaller one without throwing away the spectral structure we care about.

**Video seeds.** Put 500 eigenvalues on screen, then ask: “Can 50 numbers tell us where the important outer eigenvalues are?” Fade to the zeros of the compressed characteristic polynomial. A second video can grow the Krylov subspace and watch Ritz values converge.

**Reading.** [Walther Ritz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walther_Ritz); [Rayleigh-Ritz method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayleigh%E2%80%93Ritz_method); [Krylov subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krylov_subspace).

## October — Paul Montel: normal families and iteration

**Function/picture.** A normal family is a family of meromorphic functions from which every sequence has a suitably convergent subsequence. The calendar connects Montel's compactness idea with later work on fixed points of iterates. Its featured picture is a high iterate of an explicitly constructed rational function from a non-normal family whose early iterates still have attracting fixed points.

**Story.** Montel's idea became foundational in one-variable complex dynamics: normality is what separates stable behavior from Julia-set behavior. This is a better story than simply defining compactness. A technical theorem about families of functions became part of the language for deciding where repeated iteration behaves predictably.

**Video seeds.** Show a family of functions under repeated iteration and ask: “What does it mean for a whole family of functions to behave normally?” Contrast a region where iterates settle with one where tiny changes explode. Use the phase portrait to make the loss of normality visible.

**Reading.** [Paul Montel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Montel); [normal family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_family); Elias Wegert, “About the cover: non-normal families and attracting fixed points,” *Computational Methods and Function Theory* 23 (2023), 17–21.

## November — Johannes Erwin Papperitz: the Riemann-Papperitz equation

**Function/picture.** A second-order Fuchsian differential equation with three regular singular points reduces, after normalization, to the hypergeometric differential equation. Ratios of independent solutions map the upper half-plane to circular triangles. Repeated Schwarz reflection extends the inverse through a tessellation; in the special `(0,0,0)` case this connects to the modular lambda function. The month shows a phase portrait obtained from this continuation.

**Story.** Papperitz studied under Felix Klein and later became a professor and rector at Bergakademie Freiberg—the same institution behind *Complex Beauties*. His 1889 work made the three-singularity differential equation explicit and studied how hypergeometric transcendents could be represented by single-valued functions. This month is a natural historical bridge between differential equations, reflection groups, and automorphic functions.

**Video seeds.** Start with one circular triangle. Reflect it across an edge, then again and again until the disk/plane tessellates. Ask: “How does a differential equation turn into this tiling?” Then reveal the inverse-function phase portrait.

**Reading.** [Riemann's differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann%27s_differential_equation); [hypergeometric function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_function); [modular lambda function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_lambda_function); J. E. Papperitz, “Ueber die Darstellung der hypergeometrischen Transcendenten durch eindeutige Functionen” (1889).

## December — Henri Poincaré: automorphic functions and Poincaré series

**Function/picture.** A function is automorphic when it is unchanged by every transformation in a discrete group acting on its domain. Poincaré series manufacture functions/forms with controlled transformation laws; quotients then give automorphic functions. The calendar uses a two-generator Kleinian group and plots a quotient of two Poincaré series.

**Story.** Poincaré reached automorphic functions while studying differential equations and discovered that groups of Möbius transformations, hyperbolic geometry, and complex functions belonged to the same problem. That is the video story: symmetry is not decoration placed on a function afterward; the group can be used to *construct* the function.

**Video seeds.** Begin with two Möbius transformations and repeatedly apply them to a point or circle. Ask: “Can we build a function that refuses to notice all of these motions?” Accumulate the Poincaré series term by term and finish on the domain-colored automorphic function. This also connects directly to later Kleinian-group/Indra's Pearls material.

**Reading.** [Henri Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincar%C3%A9); [automorphic form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_form); [Poincaré series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_series_(modular_form)); [Kleinian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleinian_group).
