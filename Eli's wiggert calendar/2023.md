## January — James Gregory: the arctangent series

**Function/picture.** Start with the power series

`arctan z = z - z^3/3 + z^5/5 - ...`

and then stop it after finitely many terms. The calendar plots a high-degree Taylor partial sum (degree 201). On the real line this looks like an approximation problem; in the complex plane the zeros and the boundary of useful approximation become a geometric object.

**Story.** James Gregory found the arctangent series in the seventeenth century; related series for π were discovered in different traditions and later associated with Leibniz. The good story is not “who was first?” but how a local power series for a function became an algorithm for π—and how complex plotting shows what a real-variable derivation hides.

**Video seeds.** “This polynomial calculates π. What does it look like away from the real line?” Grow the partial sum degree and watch its zeros organize. A second video can compare the partial sum to the actual `arctan z` and make the radius/obstruction to convergence visible.

**Reading.** [James Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Gregory_(mathematician)); [inverse trigonometric functions / series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_trigonometric_functions); Ranjan Roy, “The Discovery of the Series Formula for π by Leibniz, Gregory and Nilakantha,” *Mathematics Magazine* 63(5) (1990), 291–306.

## February — Stephen Butterworth: a maximally flat filter

**Function/picture.** A Butterworth low-pass filter is chosen so its magnitude response is as flat as possible near zero frequency and then rolls off smoothly. The calendar uses a seventeenth-order transfer function with cutoff frequency 3 and looks at the rational function in the complex plane, where its poles encode the filter.

**Story.** Butterworth was a British physicist and engineer whose work ranged from electrical standards to torpedoes, submarine cables, mines, and ship degaussing. His 1930 paper produced the filter that still carries his name. The useful lesson is that “flat frequency response” is an extremal property of a complex rational function, not merely an audio-engineering curve.

**Video seeds.** First show the familiar real frequency-response curve and ask: “Where is the rest of the function?” Rotate into the complex plane and reveal the pole pattern. Another video can increase filter order and show the real response flatten while the poles fill their characteristic arc.

**Reading.** [Stephen Butterworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Butterworth); [Butterworth filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterworth_filter); Stephen Butterworth, “On the Theory of Filter Amplifiers,” *Experimental Wireless and the Wireless Engineer* 7 (1930), 536–541.

## March — Johann Bernoulli: `z^z`

**Function/picture.** For positive real `x`, `x^x` is already unusual. For complex `z`,

`z^z = exp(z log z)`

inherits the multivaluedness of the complex logarithm. The calendar shows the principal branch and compares other branches; the branch cut becomes visible rather than being a bookkeeping convention.

**Story.** Bernoulli studied `x^x` in 1697 before the exponential function had its modern status. He found its minimum and evaluated the integral from 0 to 1, leading to the identity now grouped with the “sophomore's dream.” The later complex version gives us a clean bridge from a seventeenth-century real curve to branches and Riemann-surface ideas.

**Video seeds.** Ask: “What is `i^i`—and why is there more than one answer?” Then show several branches of `z^z` side by side. Or begin with the smooth real curve `x^x` and pull the camera into the complex plane until the branch cut appears.

**Reading.** [Johann Bernoulli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Bernoulli); [sophomore's dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophomore%27s_dream); [complex logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_logarithm); William Dunham, *The Calculus Gallery* (2005), for historical background.

## April — Josephine Mitchell: Hardy-space derivatives

**Function/picture.** Hardy spaces organize analytic functions by the size of their boundary means. Mitchell and Kyong T. Hahn studied, among other things, when information about `f'` in one Hardy space forces `f` to lie in another. The calendar builds explicit functions from roots of unity,

`f(z) = z^m sum_k (1-w_k z)^(-1/2)`,

and features a phase portrait of the derivative for a particular choice of `m` and `n`.

**Story.** Josephine Mitchell built a career in complex analysis while she and her husband, mathematician Lowell Schoenfeld, faced the two-body problem of finding institutions willing to employ both of them. She held an Institute for Advanced Study fellowship, later became a full professor, and left substantial support for mathematical libraries and research. The mathematics gives a better angle than a generic “woman in mathematics” profile: boundary behavior of holomorphic functions can be tested with explicit examples whose singularities sit symmetrically around the unit circle.

**Video seeds.** Put four roots of unity on the circle, attach one square-root singular term to each, and ask: “What happens when we differentiate the whole symmetric construction?” Show `f` and `f'` together. A follow-up can change `m` or the number of roots and compare the boundary blow-up.

**Reading.** [Hardy space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy_space); [Josephine Mitchell — Wikipedia search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Search?search=Josephine+Mitchell+mathematician); the calendar points to Mitchell and Kyong T. Hahn's 1975 work on Hardy spaces and derivatives.

## May — Willem Kapteyn: Bessel series and Kepler's equation

**Function/picture.** Solving Kepler's equation for planetary motion leads to a Fourier expansion whose coefficients are Bessel functions. Kapteyn systematically studied series of the form

`sum a_n J_n(n z)`.

The calendar plots the fiftieth partial sum of the Kapteyn series arising from Kepler's equation and shows zeros clustering near the boundary of its convergence region.

**Story.** A line from astronomy to complex analysis runs through Kepler, Bessel, and Kapteyn: orbital position creates a transcendental equation; Bessel functions solve it in series; Kapteyn asks what these Bessel-function series do as complex functions. Modern work generalizing Jentzsch's theorem explains the visible zero clustering.

**Video seeds.** Start with an ellipse and the question “Given the time, where is the planet?” Then show the Bessel-series answer and finally its complex phase portrait. Another Short can increase the partial-sum order and watch zeros migrate toward the convergence boundary.

**Reading.** [Willem Kapteyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Search?search=Willem+Kapteyn+mathematician); [Kepler's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler%27s_equation); [Bessel function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_function); Folkmar Bornemann, “A Jentzsch-Theorem for Kapteyn, Neumann, and General Dirichlet Series,” arXiv:2107.07207.

## June — Yegor Zolotarev: best rational approximation to `sign(x)`

**Function/picture.** Zolotarev asked for the rational function of a fixed degree that best approximates the sign function on two separated real intervals. The optimal solutions—Zolotarev fractions—can be written with elliptic functions and have a nesting/composition property reminiscent of Chebyshev polynomials.

**Story.** Zolotarev worked with Chebyshev and produced major results in approximation theory, algebraic numbers, quadratic forms, and elliptic integrals in a very short career. He died after being struck by a train in 1878. The mathematical story is stronger: an ugly engineering-sounding minimax problem turns out to have an exact solution built from elliptic-function geometry.

**Video seeds.** Draw `sign(x)` only on two intervals, leaving a gap around zero, and ask: “What is the best possible rational fake of this jump?” Show the equioscillation points, then the domain-colored rational function. A second video can compose two Zolotarev fractions and reveal the nesting visually.

**Reading.** [Yegor Ivanovich Zolotarev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yegor_Ivanovich_Zolotarev); [rational approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_approximation); E. I. Zolotarev, “Application of Elliptic Functions to Questions on Functions Deviating Least and Most from Zero” (1877); A. B. Bogatyrev, work on Zolotarev fractions and rational Chebyshev representations.

## July — August Ferdinand Möbius: iterating a Möbius transformation

**Function/picture.** A Möbius transformation

`T(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)`

acts on the Riemann sphere and sends generalized circles to generalized circles. The calendar chooses one transformation with two fixed points, follows the forward/backward orbit of a point, and then constructs a nonconstant function invariant under the cyclic group generated by `T`.

**Story.** Möbius's name is spread across apparently unrelated objects: Möbius transformations, the Möbius function and inversion in number theory, homogeneous coordinates, and the Möbius strip. Here the right story is the group action. Repeatedly applying one fractional-linear map creates a discrete symmetry, and an automorphic function can be built to ignore that motion.

**Video seeds.** Drop one point into the plane and repeatedly apply `T`; watch it spiral from one fixed point toward the other. Then ask: “Can a function give the same value all along this orbit?” Reveal the invariant phase portrait. This is a clean precursor to the 2024 Poincaré/Kleinian entry.

**Reading.** [August Ferdinand Möbius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Ferdinand_M%C3%B6bius); [Möbius transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6bius_transformation); [automorphic form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_form).

## August — Wilhelm Cauer: elliptic filters

**Function/picture.** An elliptic/Cauer filter is an equiripple rational filter: the response oscillates with equal-sized ripples in its passband and stopband and makes the transition between them exceptionally steep for a fixed order. Its transfer function is built using the same elliptic-function approximation machinery that appears in the June Zolotarev entry.

**Story.** Wilhelm Cauer was a major figure in network synthesis and an early architect of system theory. His work shows a direct path from nineteenth-century approximation theory to twentieth-century electrical engineering. The calendar also notes the difficult political context of his career under Nazism and his death in Berlin in April 1945; that history should be handled separately from the technical explanation rather than reduced to trivia.

**Video seeds.** Compare Butterworth and elliptic filters with the same order: “How much steeper can we make this wall if we allow ripples?” Then expose the rational transfer function's poles and zeros. A paired February/August video could be especially effective.

**Reading.** [Wilhelm Cauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Cauer); [elliptic filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_filter); [network synthesis filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_synthesis_filters); Zolotarev's 1877 approximation paper is the mathematical ancestor.

## September — Harold Widom: Toeplitz determinants and the Barnes G-function

**Function/picture.** Toeplitz matrices have constant diagonals, with entries supplied by Fourier coefficients of a function on the unit circle. Their determinants have rich asymptotic behavior. In a Fisher-Hartwig example, the exact asymptotics bring in the Barnes G-function. The calendar plots `G(1+beta)G(1-beta)` in the complex `beta`-plane.

**Story.** Widom was a central figure in Toeplitz and Wiener-Hopf operator theory and later worked with Craig Tracy on random matrices. The visual lesson is a surprising one: begin with a determinant of larger and larger structured matrices, take `n -> infinity`, and a special complex function appears as the constant factor.

**Video seeds.** Build Toeplitz matrices of sizes 5, 20, 100 from the same symbol and ask: “What survives when the matrix becomes enormous?” Transition from determinants to the Barnes-G expression, then show its phase portrait.

**Reading.** [Harold Widom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Widom); [Toeplitz matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toeplitz_matrix); [Fisher-Hartwig conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher%E2%80%93Hartwig_conjecture); H. Widom, “Toeplitz determinants with singular generating functions,” *American Journal of Mathematics* 95 (1973), 333–383.

## October — Ada Lovelace: Bernoulli numbers as a program and as a function

**Function/picture.** Lovelace's Note G describes an algorithm for computing Bernoulli numbers on Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine. The calendar then moves from algorithm to complex function: the Bernoulli numbers appear as coefficients in an asymptotic expansion involving the trigamma function, and the corresponding analytic expression is phase-plotted.

**Story.** The famous “first programmer” label is only the entrance. What makes the month mathematically useful is that the same sequence of Bernoulli numbers has two representations separated by 180 years: a step-by-step machine algorithm and an analytic generating/asymptotic function. We can show both and ask what each representation is good for.

**Video seeds.** Put a few lines/table rows from Lovelace's Bernoulli-number procedure beside the modern sequence, then ask: “What does the *function* containing all these numbers look like?” Transition to the trigamma-based phase portrait. Another Short can compare recursion versus generating functions as two ways to package a sequence.

**Reading.** [Ada Lovelace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Lovelace); [Bernoulli number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_number); [trigamma function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigamma_function); L. F. Menabrea and Ada Lovelace, “Sketch of the Analytical Engine invented by Charles Babbage, with notes by the translator” (1843): https://www.fourmilab.ch/babbage/sketch.html

## November — Pekka Myrberg: period doubling in quadratic iteration

**Function/picture.** Myrberg studied iteration of quadratic polynomials reducible to

`f(z)=z^2+p`.

As `p` changes, an attracting fixed point loses stability, then attracting cycles of periods 2, 4, 8, ... appear, accumulating near the parameter now associated with the onset of chaos. The calendar phase-plots a high iterate minus its attracting fixed point for one parameter value.

**Story.** Julia and Fatou established the general theory of rational iteration, but Myrberg showed decades later that even the quadratic case still contained deep unanswered structure. This is an excellent historical correction to the idea that the Mandelbrot/Feigenbaum story simply appeared in the 1970s and 80s.

**Video seeds.** Hold the initial point fixed and slide `p`: one attracting point becomes a 2-cycle, then 4, then 8. Do not explain “chaos” first—let the viewer watch the orbit split. A second video can compare the ordinary real bifurcation picture with the complex domain-colored iterate.

**Reading.** [Pekka Myrberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Search?search=Pekka+Myrberg); [quadratic map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_map); [period-doubling bifurcation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period-doubling_bifurcation); [complex dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_dynamics).

## December — H. S. M. Coxeter: symmetry groups and automorphic functions

**Function/picture.** The rotational symmetries of the icosahedron form a finite group acting by Möbius transformations on the Riemann sphere. Average/sum a seed function over the group and the result is automatically invariant. The calendar uses a pole-type seed and phase-plots the resulting icosahedrally symmetric automorphic function.

**Story.** Coxeter spent his career making geometry and symmetry systematic, influencing work far outside pure geometry; Buckminster Fuller's geodesic domes and M. C. Escher's hyperbolic *Circle Limit* prints are familiar examples. The function gives us a precise way to say what symmetry means: not “it looks symmetric,” but `f(g(z))=f(z)` for every transformation `g` in the group.

**Video seeds.** Put an icosahedron on the sphere, rotate it through one generator, and ask: “Can we make a complex function with exactly this symmetry?” Sum transformed copies of one simple pole and watch the symmetry appear. This is a natural lead-in to the 2024 Poincaré-series entry.

**Reading.** [H. S. M. Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._S._M._Coxeter); [icosahedral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icosahedral_symmetry); [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action); Coxeter obituary, *Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society* 41 (2009), 943–960.
