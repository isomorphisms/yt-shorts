# Selective Short seeds

This is a **working visual shortlist**, not a plan to exhaust Euclid proposition by proposition. Add an episode when there is a concrete mathematical point to make, an unusually clean visual move, or a piece that will be useful as a reusable dependency for a later explanation.

For every item below, Byrne's 1847 edition supplies the colored visual vocabulary; David Joyce's Clark University edition is the default dependency/commentary check.

## Priority A — unusually good standalone pieces

### Book I

#### I.1 — construct an equilateral triangle

- **Visual move:** draw a segment, then two circles with that segment as radius; their intersection completes the triangle.
- **Why keep it:** almost no verbal setup, and it establishes compass-circle intersection as a reusable construction primitive.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI1.html

#### I.32 — the angles of a triangle add to two right angles

- **Visual move:** extend one side and draw a parallel through the opposite vertex so the three triangle angles visibly reassemble along one straight line.
- **Why keep it:** a compact example of a proof becoming almost literal when the auxiliary parallel appears.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI32.html

#### I.35 — shear a parallelogram without changing its area

- **Visual move:** keep the same base and the same pair of parallels while sliding the opposite side sideways.
- **Why keep it:** this is a reusable geometric intuition for area-preserving shear; it can later support determinants, linear algebra, and Cavalieri-style arguments.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI35.html

#### I.47 — Pythagorean theorem

- **Visual move:** build the three squares and animate the auxiliary lines that partition the large square areas into the pieces Euclid compares.
- **Why keep it:** familiar theorem, unfamiliar proof; Byrne's colored organization is particularly well suited to showing which areas are being identified.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI47.html

### Book II

#### II.4 — the square on a sum

- **Visual move:** split a square of side `a+b` into `a²`, `b²`, and the two `ab` rectangles.
- **Why keep it:** algebra appears as literal colored area rather than symbolic manipulation.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookII/propII4.html

#### II.11 — cut a line in the golden ratio

- **Visual move:** follow Euclid's construction step by step until the original segment is divided in extreme and mean ratio.
- **Why keep it:** connects geometric algebra, the golden ratio, and the later regular-pentagon construction.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookII/propII11.html

### Book III

#### III.1 — find the center of a given circle

- **Visual move:** choose a chord, bisect it, and extend the perpendicular bisector through the circle.
- **Why keep it:** inverse construction: the circle is already there and the hidden center is recovered from visible data.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIII/propIII1.html

#### III.20 — central angle is twice the inscribed angle

- **Visual move:** hold the same arc while displaying the angle at the center and the angle on the circumference.
- **Why keep it:** excellent candidate for a moving-point animation in which the numerical relation remains fixed.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIII/propIII20.html

#### III.31 — angle in a semicircle is a right angle

- **Visual move:** keep the diameter fixed and move the third vertex along the semicircle; the angle stays 90°.
- **Why keep it:** a theorem that becomes almost experimental under animation, followed by a very short geometric explanation.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIII/propIII31.html

#### III.35 — intersecting chords

- **Visual move:** move the intersection point or one chord while displaying that the two segment products remain equal.
- **Why keep it:** a concrete precursor to power of a point.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIII/propIII35.html

#### III.36 — tangent-secant relation

- **Visual move:** compare the square on the tangent length with the rectangle formed by the whole secant and its external part.
- **Why keep it:** makes the second standard power-of-a-point configuration visible immediately after III.35.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIII/propIII36.html

### Book IV

#### IV.11 — inscribe a regular pentagon

- **Visual move:** construct the special isosceles triangle, transfer its angles around the circle, and watch the pentagon close.
- **Why keep it:** a beautiful end point for the golden-ratio material from Book II and a reusable classical-construction piece.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIV/propIV11.html

#### IV.15 — inscribe a regular hexagon

- **Visual move:** step the circle's radius around its circumference six times.
- **Why keep it:** the side of the regular hexagon equals the radius, so the construction explains itself visually.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIV/propIV15.html

### Book VI

#### VI.2 — a parallel cuts the sides of a triangle proportionally

- **Visual move:** slide a line parallel to one side through a triangle while corresponding side ratios remain equal.
- **Why keep it:** similarity and proportionality in their most reusable animated form.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookVI/propVI2.html

#### VI.8 — altitude to the hypotenuse creates three similar triangles

- **Visual move:** drop the altitude from the right-angle vertex and recolor the three resulting similar triangles so corresponding angles/sides line up.
- **Why keep it:** one diagram generates similarity, geometric-mean relations, and another route into the Pythagorean theorem.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookVI/propVI8.html

#### VI.30 — cut a line in extreme and mean ratio

- **Visual move:** show the ratio relation directly on the divided segment and connect it back to the construction in II.11.
- **Why keep it:** a clean reusable golden-ratio primitive.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookVI/propVI30.html

#### VI.31 — generalized Pythagorean theorem

- **Visual move:** replace the three squares with any three similar figures built on the sides of a right triangle; the two smaller areas still sum to the larger.
- **Why keep it:** substantially broadens what viewers mean when they think they already know the Pythagorean theorem.
- Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookVI/propVI31.html

## Priority B — useful dependencies and follow-ups

### Book I

- **I.9 — bisect an angle:** construction primitive for many later constructions. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI9.html
- **I.10 — bisect a segment:** another small construction primitive. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI10.html
- **I.20 — triangle inequality:** animate a broken route versus the direct side. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI20.html
- **I.37 — triangles on the same base and between the same parallels have equal area:** triangle version of the shear idea. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI37.html
- **I.48 — converse of Pythagoras:** side lengths force the right angle rather than the other way around. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI48.html

### Book II

- **II.1 — distributive law as area:** one rectangle split into several rectangles. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookII/propII1.html
- **II.14 — square equal in area to a given rectilinear figure:** transform a general shape into an equal-area square through Euclid's construction chain. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookII/propII14.html

### Book III

- **III.16 — tangent and the “horn angle”:** useful partly because the limiting-looking configuration raises a real conceptual question about angle magnitude. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIII/propIII16.html
- **III.21 — angles in the same segment are equal:** move the vertex along one arc while the subtended chord remains fixed. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIII/propIII21.html

### Book IV

- **IV.4 — inscribe a circle in a triangle:** angle bisectors locate the incenter. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIV/propIV4.html
- **IV.6 — inscribe a square in a circle:** perpendicular diameters supply the four vertices. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIV/propIV6.html
- **IV.16 — inscribe a regular 15-gon:** combine already-constructible arcs from the triangle and pentagon. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookIV/propIV16.html

### Book V

Book V is less naturally Byrne/Manim-friendly because the mathematics is an abstract theory of ratio, but several items are important enough to keep available.

- **Definition V.5 — Eudoxus' equality of ratios:** compare all equimultiples rather than assuming numerical fractions; this is the route that handles incommensurable magnitudes. Joyce Book V: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookV/bookV.html
- **V.15 — ratios survive equal multiplication:** `c:f = nc:nf`; potentially animate repeated copies rather than symbols alone. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookV/propV15.html
- **V.22 — ex aequali:** a chain of proportional magnitudes collapses to the ratio of the endpoints. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookV/propV22.html

### Book VI

- **VI.13 — construct a mean proportional:** a semicircle construction turns `a:x = x:b` into a visible right-triangle altitude. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookVI/propVI13.html
- **VI.16 — proportion as equal rectangle areas:** `a:b = c:d` becomes equality of the rectangles on the extremes and means. Joyce: https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookVI/propVI16.html

## Seed selection rule

A proposition should move upward in priority when at least one of these is true:

- one auxiliary line suddenly makes the result visible;
- a point or line can move while an invariant remains obvious;
- the proposition is a dependency that later explanations would otherwise have to recreate repeatedly;
- Byrne's color substitutions clarify the proof better than conventional letter-heavy notation;
- there is a specific mathematical or historical point worth saying, rather than merely a pretty diagram.
