## Strong first batch

### Chapter 5 — Measuring Heaven and Earth, p. 63

Obvious family of Shorts: triangulation and indirect measurement. Fix a baseline that can actually be measured, sight an inaccessible point from its endpoints, and let the angles determine the remaining distances.

Visual experiments:

- measure the height of a tower/tree from a known horizontal distance and elevation angle;
- triangulate an inaccessible point across water from a measured baseline;
- exaggerate a tiny angular error and show how it changes a distant inferred position;
- make the triangle appear on top of the landscape only after the measurement setup is clear.

Read the chapter before attaching a particular historical survey or quotation to Maor.

### Chapter 7 — Epicycloids and Hypocycloids, p. 95

This is an unusually strong animation chapter. A smaller circle rolls around the outside or inside of a larger circle while a marked point traces the curve. A contemporary teaching note explicitly describes Maor's chapter this way and notes the connection to Spirograph-style motion.

Visual experiments:

- keep the two circle centers and the marked tracing radius visible;
- roll rather than merely plot the parametric curve;
- compare epi- and hypocycloid with the same radii;
- change the radius ratio and watch cusps/symmetry change;
- for rational ratios, stop when the curve closes.

Secondary source confirming the rolling-circle content: https://lostmathlessons.blogspot.com/2022/02/epicycloids-and-hypocycloids.html

### Chapter 10 — `(sin x)/x`, p. 129

Strong visual possibilities, but read the chapter before claiming which one is Maor's central route.

Candidate experiments:

- unit-circle / sector geometry squeezing `sin x / x` toward 1 near zero;
- graph the function while zooming into the removable hole at zero;
- compare `sin x`, `x`, and `tan x` geometrically for small positive angles;
- later, if the chapter supports it, connect the same shape to another application rather than importing one from elsewhere.

### Jules Lissajous and His Figures, p. 145

This short interlude is almost pre-adapted to animation.

Start from perpendicular harmonic motions

`x(t) = sin(a t + δ)`

`y(t) = sin(b t)`

and let their combination draw the plane curve.

Visual experiments:

- show the horizontal and vertical oscillators separately, then couple their moving coordinates into one point;
- sweep simple frequency ratios such as 1:1, 1:2, 2:3, 3:4;
- hold the frequency ratio fixed and change only phase;
- leave the completed curve behind after one closed period.

Publisher chapter record: https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781400846757.145/html

### Chapter 12 — `tan x`, p. 150

This chapter should be read alongside the [laser-pointer](laser-pointer-tangent.md) note. Tangent is especially good for a physical animation because a steadily rotating ray hits a fixed line at `x = d tan θ`, visibly turning equal angle increments into unequal linear increments.

Possible paired Short:

- left side: unit-circle / tangent-line geometry;
- right side: laser pointer and wall;
- keep the same `θ` controlling both pictures;
- approach 90° and let both tangent constructions run out of frame.

Do not assume that this exact laser example occurs in Maor; use the book chapter as a tangent source and the laser as our own applied visualization.

### Chapter 15 — Fourier's Theorem, p. 198

A natural later sequence is to build a non-sinusoidal periodic signal by adding one sine/cosine component at a time.

Visual experiments:

- start with one sinusoid;
- add harmonics one at a time while keeping a small stack of component waves visible;
- show the sum becoming a sharper periodic shape;
- pair a rotating-phasor view with the time-domain sum only if it remains legible on a phone screen.

Read the chapter before deciding which theorem statement/history Maor emphasizes and before turning this into finished copy.
